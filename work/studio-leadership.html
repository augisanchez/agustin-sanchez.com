Leading design,
level by level

Design leadership and ops Led the studio · 4 designers 2026

Leading a design studio through a change in leadership: what people needed at each level, and how leading by example and teaching built a culture where it was safe to say the true thing.

The studio's head of design left in August, after a year in which the team had already watched colleagues go. Four designers, split between the US and the UK, were all asking the same question in different words: is design being wound down? I took on the whole job: the people, the practice and its operations, and design's place at the company's leadership table. I stayed billable on my own client work the whole way through.

I think about leadership in levels. Each level serves a different human need, and each is a place where culture gets built or quietly lost. What follows is how I worked at each one.

Safety first.
Then standards.

People do their best work when it's safe to say what's actually true: that they're worried, that they're stuck, that a plan is wrong. Standards only stick on top of that. So the order mattered. Safety first, then the standard, then the work.

Level 1 · Leading yourself

Lead by example.

The need here is credibility. A team takes its cue from what its leader actually does, not what they say. So I kept making things: I stayed billable on client work, and when I wanted to make a case I built a working prototype instead of a slide.

I used AI in my own client work before I asked the team to grow their fluency with it. Asking for a standard you don't hold yourself is the fastest way to teach that standards are optional.

Level 2 · Leading individuals

Grow the person, not the role.

The need here is growth, and being seen. Every designer had a recurring 1:1 with me on their work, their workload and how they were holding up, and those conversations shaped the staffing, not the other way round.

Three of the four wanted to move from execution toward strategy, and each named the same blocker: what they got staffed on. I treated it as one problem with three faces and placed each designer on work matched to where they wanted to grow. Two of the four now lead engagements as the client-facing design lead. In a review earlier that year, one designer wrote: "I have never felt this level of support from a manager."

Level 3 · Leading the team

Make it safe to say the true thing.

The need here is safety and belonging. I promised the team that nothing would be restructured in the first 30 days, and I kept that promise, because a promise kept early is what makes the next hard conversation possible.

Before I had a plan, I ran a health check in two sessions, one per time zone. We talked first and wrote things down afterwards, and the record carried no names. The point was to let people say what they wouldn't say with their name attached. I then moved the studio onto one shared weekly sync that worked for both time zones, so both halves of the team heard things at the same time.

Level 4 · Leading the practice

Teach the standard, then write it down.

The need here is clarity: knowing what good looks like and how to grow. The ladder had no design leadership level, and the competency tool had grown into a checklist of 300-plus items that nobody could use to answer "what does it take to get promoted?" I wrote a unified eight-level career framework with competencies and an explicit expectation for working with AI, took it to the team first, then to leadership and HR.

Education was the operating model, not an extra. I designed a six-course AI capability curriculum built for how designers work, set a shared course of study as the team's baseline, and ran staffing and allocation as a regular rhythm instead of a scramble. My read, not a measured result: a standard people were taught is one they hold each other to. A standard that's only enforced is one they work around.

Level 5 · Leading across the company

Earn design its seat.

The need here is for the studio's work to matter to the business. I kept regular time with the company's leadership and brought design into the pursuits that pay for the studio: proposals, pricing conversations, and a live AI design demo for a healthcare platform's executive team, which then chose us.

Two design-led engagements were won in two months, with a third client choosing us and the contract in progress. Every one of them gave a designer on the team work they wanted to grow into, which is how the top level feeds back into the first.

Hindsight

Scope before work.

I'd put the role's scope in writing on day one. I took on the whole job and kept the old title, and nobody, me included, wrote down what the new job was. Leadership work got the leftovers of the calendar while client work took the mornings. The team never felt that, because I made sure of it. The person who did feel it was me. Leading by example includes this part: next time the scope, the decision rights and the time come before the work, not after it.
