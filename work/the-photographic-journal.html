The Photographic
Journal

Publishing Founded 2012, Los Angeles Ongoing

An independent photography journal I co-founded in 2012 and still publish. A community of photographers, a long-running interview practice, and a platform for sharing work, now being rebuilt.

Visit the journal ↗

The first interview ran on October 25, 2012: me, interviewing my friend Lou Noble. Lou went on to conduct interviews himself and now runs the publication as editor-in-chief. Thirteen years later the journal has published 723 pieces: 605 photo essays, 98 interviews and 20 features, with a masthead of eight and submissions that have never closed.

Three things make it work, and they're the three things this page is about: a community of photographers who publish with us and come back, a research practice built on long-form interviews, and a platform designed so their work can be found.

Community

A community of photographers.

Most of what we publish comes from photographers who submit their own work. The photo essays come from contributors around the world, through an open submission process that has stayed open since the first year. The editorial job is to give that work a careful home.

Photographers come back. Emily Shur first sat with us in 2014 and returned eleven years later. Elinor Carucci and Olivia Bee have each been interviewed twice. When Kate Sweeney publishes with us the credit reads "x TPJ", because the relationship became a collaboration.

The community reaches past the site. The journal has collaborated with Polaroid, Lomography, the Smithsonian and Vans, and Flickr's official blog featured the interviews as a recurring series.

Research

98 interviews, one method.

The interviews are the journal's research practice. Each one is a long, open conversation about how a photographer actually works: how they find subjects, make decisions, earn trust, and live with the work afterwards. Read together, they're closer to ethnography than to press: a record of a working community, in its own words, over thirteen years.

The method rewards patience. Chris Buck put it best about his own interview: "I say things here that I usually keep to myself." The archive includes David LaChapelle, Gregory Crewdson, Alec Soth, Duane Michals, Roger Ballen and Martin Schoeller. I conducted the LaChapelle interview myself in January 2026. A Joel Meyerowitz interview is in progress, with questions written to avoid the origin stories he's already told many times.

The same research habit shaped the rebuild. Before designing anything, we read back through all 723 pieces and derived eleven themes from what was actually in the archive, so the new structure reflects what the community actually made.

One platform.

In 2026 I'm rebuilding the journal as a platform for sharing work: photographers become real pages, the archive becomes browsable by the people and themes in it, and the hosting costs come down to something an independent publication can carry. I'm the designer and builder; Lou runs editorial. It's in progress.

Platform

Built so the work can be found.

The old site knew what it had published and almost nothing about what was in it. There was no photographer field, no medium, no location. For a publication about people, you couldn't browse it by the people in it.

The new platform makes every photographer a page of their own, with their essays and interviews gathered in one place and credits that support more than one photographer per story. The archive can be browsed by theme, genre and medium. Editors own every theme's wording in the content system, so the structure can grow without a developer.

Built to last

Sized for two people to run.

WordPress stays as the content system, where thirteen years of editorial habits live, and a Next.js front end handles the reading experience. Every technical choice answered one question: can two people maintain this?

Before building, I audited a full copy of the production database instead of trusting the planning documents, and changed the plan wherever the data disagreed. Moving image delivery off the WordPress host is projected to take hosting from about $92 a month to about $7. An accessibility pass with two independent checkers took the core templates from 89 errors to zero.
