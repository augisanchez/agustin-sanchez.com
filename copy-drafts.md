## LEADERSHIP INTRO — BODY COPY

Three paragraphs. IBM Plex Sans 300, 58–64ch max-width.
Sticky headline on the left: DESIGN IS NOT JUST ABOUT WHAT WE MAKE.

---

### Version A

**Paragraph 1 — The translation problem**
The organizations I've worked with don't stall because of bad ideas. They stall because the right people can't see the same thing at the same time. Design's real value isn't prettier interfaces or more research decks — it's the ability to make complex problems legible enough that the right people can make the right calls. That's translation. It's harder than it looks, and most organizations don't have anyone who does it well.

**Paragraph 2 — AI and the shift to judgment**
AI is changing what's worth paying for. The ability to generate outputs — artifacts, documents, variations, analyses — is becoming a commodity. What remains scarce is the judgment to know which problem is worth solving, the clarity to frame it in a way that aligns a room, and the courage to make a call when the signals are mixed. That's what twenty years of this work builds. It's more valuable now than it's ever been.

**Paragraph 3 — Creative cross-training**
Photography taught me to see what's actually there, not what I expected to find. Music taught me to listen across registers simultaneously — to hear the whole while staying in the part. Writing taught me to keep thinking until the thought was clear. I didn't set out to build a practice out of these things. But they've made me a better design leader than any methodology has. They keep me calibrated to what's real when everything inside an organization is moving fast.

---

### Version B (slightly warmer, more personal)

**Paragraph 1 — The translation problem**
Most of what I do doesn't look like design from the outside. It looks like facilitating the right conversation, making a decision visible that was previously invisible, or helping a room understand that they've been solving the wrong problem. The craft underneath all of that is design — but the outcome is organizational clarity. That's the work I care about.

**Paragraph 2 — AI and the shift to judgment**
I've spent the last few years watching AI reshape what execution costs. It's changing the economics of delivery faster than most organizations have adjusted to. But it's making one thing more valuable, not less: the ability to frame the right problem, hold competing priorities in tension, and build enough shared understanding that a team can actually move. That's a human skill. It's the skill I've spent twenty years building.

**Paragraph 3 — Creative cross-training**
I make photographs. I make music. I write. Not as a side pursuit — as a deliberate practice that keeps my perception sharp. Shooting film trains patience and specificity. Mixing trains listening across multiple registers at once. Writing trains clarity — you can't hide vague thinking in a sentence. These aren't hobbies. They're how I stay calibrated to what's real when organizations need someone to see clearly.

---

### Version C (most declarative — closest to executive register)

**Paragraph 1 — The translation problem**
Design's defensible value has never been the artifacts it produces. It's the ability to make a complex problem legible — to translate between what's true about users, what's feasible for engineering, and what's viable for the business, and do it in a way that drives decisions rather than debates. Twenty years in, that's still the work. AI changes the tools. It doesn't change what the work is.

**Paragraph 2 — AI and the shift to judgment**
Production is becoming a commodity. The value that remains is judgment: knowing which problem deserves the effort, how to align the room around a direction, and when to push past the comfortable answer toward the true one. Organizations are going to need fewer people who execute well and more people who can think clearly under ambiguity. That shift is already underway.

**Paragraph 3 — Creative cross-training**
Photography, music, and writing are not separable from how I work. They are how I train the underlying skills that organizational work demands: noticing what's actually in front of you, listening across multiple signals at once, and thinking until you have something worth saying. Creative cross-training isn't a lifestyle choice. It's a leadership practice.

## ORGANIZATIONAL WORK — BODY COPY

Two columns. `rgba(244,242,238,0.65)` — muted paper on ink background.
Follows the THEY STALL WHEN ALIGNMENT BREAKS DOWN headlines.

---

### Version A

**Left column — The problem**
Most teams I work with are not confused about what to build. They're stuck in a loop of competing priorities, undefined tradeoffs, and conversations that end without decisions. The problem isn't bad ideas or bad people. It's a failure of translation — between strategy and execution, between disciplines, between what leadership believes and what the team understands.

**Right column — The approach**
I work at that gap. The method is less about process and more about signal — making visible what's actually true about user behavior, technical feasibility, and business viability, and using that clarity to drive decisions rather than debates. When those three signals align, organizations stop stalling and start moving.

---

### Version B (more specific, names the dynamics)

**Left column — The problem**
Alignment doesn't break down because people disagree. It breaks down because the conversation that would surface the disagreement never happens. Teams build momentum around a shared assumption that was never actually tested. By the time the misalignment is visible, significant work has already been done in the wrong direction.

**Right column — The approach**
The most valuable thing I can do in an organization is make the invisible decision visible — name the assumption that's been treated as a fact, surface the tradeoff that's been avoided, create the conditions for a real choice. That's not a design skill. It's an organizational one. Design is what I use to make it legible.

---

### Version C (shortest — most direct)

**Left column — The problem**
Bad alignment is rarely about conflict. It's about the critical conversations that never happened — the assumptions that went unchallenged, the tradeoffs that were deferred, the problems that looked solved but weren't. By the time it's visible, the waste is already done.

**Right column — The approach**
I help organizations catch it earlier. Not by adding process — by creating moments of real clarity: a shared view of what's true, what's uncertain, and what a good decision looks like. Design is the instrument. Alignment is the outcome.

## PRINCIPLES — BODY COPY

Essay style: Source Serif 4 italic. 3–5 sentences per block.
These are beliefs, not product copy.

---

### CRAFT — CRAFT IS NECESSARY. IT'S JUST NOT ENOUGH.

**Version A**
Craft is the price of entry. It earns you a seat at the table and the right to be taken seriously. But I've watched brilliant craftspeople produce work that didn't move anything — because they optimized the artifact instead of the outcome. The organizations that need you most aren't asking for better design. They're asking for better decisions, better alignment, and less wasted motion. Craft is what you use to get there. It's not the destination.

**Version B**
I spent the first decade of my career trying to make things as good as they could possibly be. That's not a bad instinct. But craft without direction is just refinement — you get very good at making the wrong thing more beautiful. The second decade taught me that the question before the craft question is always: what would make this matter?

**Version C (shortest)**
Craft earns you entry. What you do once you're inside — how you think, what you prioritize, whether you can help a room make a real decision — that's what determines impact. The two skills are related but not the same. Most designers train one. The ones who lead well have built both.

---

### COURAGE — THE BEST ANSWERS LIVE IN UNCOMFORTABLE PLACES.

**Version A**
The safest recommendation is always a variation on what already exists. Organizations have gravity — toward the familiar, toward the consensus option, toward the answer that doesn't require anyone to change much. I've learned that the most useful thing you can do in a room is name what everyone is avoiding. Not to be provocative. Because that's usually where the real problem is, and the real problem is always worth solving.

**Version B**
There's a kind of answer that gets arrived at too quickly — the one that resolves the tension in the room without resolving the actual problem. I've been in enough of those rooms to recognize the pattern. The real answer usually requires someone to say something that makes the room go quiet for a moment. That discomfort is signal. It means you've found the edge worth working at.

**Version C (most direct)**
Consensus is not the same as clarity. The most useful thing a design leader can do is name what the room is avoiding. Not to be difficult — because the avoided thing is almost always the real problem, and the real problem is the only one worth solving. Courage in organizational work mostly looks like precision: being specific about what's true when everyone else is being vague.

---

### CURIOSITY — STAY IN THE QUESTION LONGER.

**Version A**
There's a pressure in organizational work to move from problem to solution as fast as possible. It feels like progress. It usually isn't. The questions that matter — why this problem now, who actually owns it, what a good outcome looks like in six months — are worth sitting in longer than feels comfortable. The best work I've done started with someone willing to slow down before they sped up.

**Version B**
Curiosity isn't a personality trait in this context. It's a discipline. The organizations I've seen move well are the ones that stay in the question long enough to understand it — before the pressure to ship turns every problem into a backlog item. Premature solutions are expensive. The investment in real understanding is almost always cheaper than the rework.

**Version C (most personal)**
I still catch myself rushing toward the answer. It's a trained reflex — organizations reward motion, and motion looks like answers. But the most valuable thing I can bring into a problem space is the willingness to sit with it longer than feels productive. The question usually has more in it than the answer does. The answer is just the beginning of the next question.

---

### THE GAP — MOST ORGANIZATIONS DON'T HAVE A DESIGN PROBLEM. / THEY HAVE A TRANSLATION PROBLEM.

**Version A**
Design can articulate a vision with more clarity than most disciplines. But clarity that lives inside the design org isn't clarity — it's isolation. The real work is getting that vision to land across engineering, product, finance, and leadership in a way each of them can act on. That's the gap. And it's rarely filled by better artifacts. It's filled by better conversations, better framing, and someone willing to sit at all the tables.

**Version B**
The most common failure mode I've seen is a design team with the right answer and no one to hear it. Not because the work isn't good. Because it was made in a language that didn't travel. The design was for users. The organization needed something it could understand and act on. Bridging that — making the insight land inside the org, not just in the file — is the job that doesn't show up in job descriptions but determines almost everything.

**Version C (most declarative)**
The artifact is not the deliverable. The decision is the deliverable. The research finding, the prototype, the service blueprint — these are means. The end is a shared understanding clear enough that the right people can act. When design fails to create that, it isn't usually a craft failure. It's a translation failure. That's the gap most design organizations are sitting in, and it's the one I've spent twenty years learning how to close.
