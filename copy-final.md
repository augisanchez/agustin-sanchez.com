## LEADERSHIP INTRO — BODY COPY

Three paragraphs. IBM Plex Sans 300, 58–64ch max-width.
Sticky headline (left): DESIGN IS NOT JUST ABOUT WHAT WE MAKE.

**Paragraph 1 — The translation problem**
Design's defensible value has never been the artifacts it produces. It's the ability to make a complex problem legible — to translate between what's true about users, what's feasible for engineering, and what's viable for the business, and do it in a way that drives decisions rather than debates. Twenty years in, that's still the work. AI changes the tools. It doesn't change what the work is.

**Paragraph 2 — AI and the shift to judgment**
Production is becoming a commodity. The value that remains is judgment: knowing which problem deserves the effort, how to align the room around a direction, and when to push past the comfortable answer toward the true one. Organizations are going to need fewer people who execute well and more people who can think clearly under ambiguity. That shift is already underway.

**Paragraph 3 — Creative cross-training**
Photography, music, and writing are not separable from how I work. They are how I train the underlying skills that organizational work demands: noticing what's actually in front of you, listening across multiple signals at once, and thinking until you have something worth saying. Creative cross-training isn't a lifestyle choice. It's a leadership practice.

## ORGANIZATIONAL WORK — BODY COPY

Two columns. `rgba(244,242,238,0.65)` on --ink background.
Follows: THEY STALL WHEN ALIGNMENT BREAKS DOWN.

**Left column — The problem**
Bad alignment is rarely about conflict. It's about the critical conversations that never happened — the assumptions that went unchallenged, the tradeoffs that were deferred, the problems that looked solved but weren't. By the time it's visible, the waste is already done.

**Right column — The approach**
I help organizations catch it earlier. Not by adding process — by creating moments of real clarity: a shared view of what's true, what's uncertain, and what a good decision looks like. Design is the instrument. Alignment is the outcome.

### CRAFT

**Headline:** THE TOOLS WILL CHANGE. YOUR CRAFT DOESN'T HAVE TO.

*(Reframed from original brief. References your Medium article directly — this is a POV you've already published, not a new claim.)*

**Body:**
AI can generate a hundred variations in the time it used to take to make one. That's not the threat people think it is. The question has never been how fast you can produce — it's whether you know which one is worth keeping. Craft, in this context, isn't about execution speed. It's taste. It's judgment. It's knowing what good looks like before the brief, before the deadline, before the stakeholder presentation. That doesn't go away. If anything, it becomes the only thing that's fully yours.

---

### COURAGE

**Headline:** THE BEST ANSWERS LIVE IN UNCOMFORTABLE PLACES.

**Body:**
Consensus is not the same as clarity. There's a kind of answer that gets arrived at too quickly — the one that resolves the tension in the room without resolving the actual problem. I've been in enough of those rooms to recognize the pattern. The real answer usually requires someone to say something that makes the room go quiet for a moment. That discomfort is signal. Courage in organizational work mostly looks like precision: being specific about what's true when everyone else is being vague.

---

### CURIOSITY

**Headline:** STAY IN THE QUESTION LONGER.

**Body:**
I still catch myself rushing toward the answer. It's a trained reflex — organizations reward motion, and motion looks like answers. But the most valuable thing I can bring into a problem space is the willingness to sit with it longer than feels productive. The question usually has more in it than the answer does. The answer is just the beginning of the next question.

---

### THE GAP

**Headline:** MOST ORGANIZATIONS DON'T HAVE A DESIGN PROBLEM. / THEY HAVE A TRANSLATION PROBLEM.

**Body:**
The artifact is not the deliverable. The decision is the deliverable. The research finding, the prototype, the service blueprint — these are means. The end is a shared understanding clear enough that the right people can act. When design fails to create that, it isn't usually a craft failure. It's a translation failure. That's the gap most design organizations are sitting in, and it's the one I've spent twenty years learning how to close.
